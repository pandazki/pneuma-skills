Humans and Code Agents Begin Sharing One Drafting Table

Work Moves Onto a Live Canvas

Designers Point, the Machines Draw, and Both Watch the Page Change as It Happens

Skeptics Ask Who Holds the Pen

By Eleanor Voss Technology Correspondent

HHarbor City, Sept. 22 — On the fourth floor of a converted cannery on Mercer Street, the designer Ada Lindqvist spent Tuesday morning doing something that a year ago would have taken her a week. She described a page to a code agent, watched it build the page on a screen they could both see, and when a headline sat wrong she did not write a ticket. She clicked on it.

“That one,” she said. The agent, which had already been told what she clicked, set the headline two sizes smaller and moved on to the next column.

Scenes like it are becoming ordinary in studios and small software shops across the city. For most of the short history of code-writing software, the work happened out of sight: a person typed a request, the machine returned a block of text, and someone had to run it to learn what it did.

The new arrangement puts both parties in front of the same live surface, whether a web page, a slide deck or a diagram, and that surface redraws itself with every edit the agent makes.

People who use these shared workspaces describe a change less in speed than in posture. The human stops reviewing finished work and starts watching work in progress, the way an editor once stood behind a compositor at the stone. They can stop a wrong turn early, point at the exact thing they mean, and hand over context a paragraph of instructions would have missed.

“The page is the conversation now,” said Marcus Oduya, who runs a four-person shop called Aldine Works. “I don’t read what it says it did. I look.”

Not everyone is persuaded. Critics warn that watching a machine is not the same as understanding it, and that a pleasant live preview can hide a sloppy file underneath.

Night Ferry to the Outer Islands Wins Final Vote

Council Approves 11 P.M. Sailings

Islanders Hail End of the Six O’Clock Curfew; Service to Begin in April

By Thomas Pell

Harbor City, Sept. 22 — After three years of petitions, two public hearings and one memorable filibuster by a councilman from Gull Island, the City Council voted 9 to 2 on Tuesday night to extend ferry service to the outer islands until 11 o’clock.

For the roughly 4,000 people who live on Gull, Tern and Little Brother islands, the last boat home now leaves Pier 4 at 6:10 p.m. Nurses on the night shift sleep on cots at the harbor hospital; cooks from the islands keep rooms in town.

“My daughter has never seen a film that did not start at four in the afternoon,” said Ruth Anand, who testified at both hearings.

The later sailings will cost the city an estimated $1.8 million a year, covered in part by a 25-cent rise in daytime fares.

Quay Street’s Last Lamplighter Puts Down His Pole

After 41 Years, Tomas Reyes Lights the Final Gas Lamp by Hand

By June Hartley

Tomas Reyes lit the 31 gas lamps on Quay Street for the last time on Tuesday evening, working from the fish market to the customs house as he has every dusk since 1985. The lamps will be fitted with timers next month.

A small crowd followed him down the street, and when he reached the final lamp somebody began to clap. Mr. Reyes, 68, said he intends to sleep through sunset for a week.

“After that,” he said, “I will probably come down and watch the timers.”

Opinion

Let the Machine Show Its Work

By Margaret Hale Columnist

When I was a copy girl at this newspaper, the most useful thing an old editor told me was to go down to the composing room and watch. Not to check the finished page, which anyone could do, but to stand at the stone while it was being made, because that was where the mistakes were small enough to fix with a thumb.

I thought of him this week reading Eleanor Voss’s report on the studios where people and code agents now build pages together in plain view. The novelty is not that the machines are faster. It is that, for the first time, we can watch them at the stone.

That is a good thing, and I want to say plainly why. Work you can see is work you can correct. A mistake caught at the third line costs a word; caught at the thirtieth page, it costs a week and a good deal of temper.

But watching carries its own temptation, which is to confuse a handsome page with a sound one. The old compositors knew a line could look straight and still be set in the wrong face. We will need the same habit: look, and then look underneath.

Printers’ Guild Votes to Keep the Old Presses Running

Plan Pairs 1962 Line With New Equipment

Members Say the Veteran Press Still Prints the Cleanest Engravings in the State

By Samuel Ortiz

Members of Local 17 of the Harbor City Printers’ Guild voted 112 to 9 on Monday to back a plan that would keep this newspaper’s 1962 rotary press in service for at least another decade, running beside a new digital line due in the spring.

The old press, which fills most of the ground floor of the Water Street plant, prints roughly 60,000 copies a night. Its rollers were last replaced in 2011.

“She is slower than anything they make now,” said Harold Beck, the night foreman, who has run the press for 27 years. “She is also the only one in the state that can hold a fine engraving without muddying it.”

A Quartet Fills the Old Exchange Hall, Then Hushes It

By Clara Wen

The Linden Quartet has a habit of starting so softly that an audience leans forward to find the music. On Monday at the Old Exchange Hall, where the acoustics were built for auctioneers, the habit became a kind of dare.

By the slow movement of the Schubert, a hall of 900 people had stopped rustling its programs. By the last chord, nobody wanted to be the first to clap.

Okafor’s Slide Beats the Tag

Harbor City Heads to Its First Series Since 1998 After a 5-4 Win Over Ridgefield

By Danny Morrow

Luis Okafor had not stolen a base since June. With two outs in the 10th inning on Tuesday night and the pennant riding on a single run, he decided that was long enough.

He broke for home on a slow roller to second, and the throw beat him by what looked, from the press box, like half a step. He slid anyway, feet first and wide, and swept a hand across the plate beneath the catcher’s mitt.

Umpire Frank Delaney held his arms out flat. The crowd of 31,000 at Pier Park took a moment to believe it.

The Valley

Rains Hold the Valley Harvest in Check

Picking Is Two Weeks Behind

Growers Wait Under Barn Roofs as the Wettest September in 30 Years Runs Long

By Nora Flint

Alder Creek, Sept. 22 — The apples are ready and the ladders are up. On Monday afternoon at the Hollis orchard, fifteen pickers sat on upturned crates under the barn roof and watched the rain come down on both.

The valley has had rain on 19 of the last 22 days, the wettest September since 1996 by the county’s count. Fruit left too long on the branch splits; fruit picked wet bruises in the crate.

“You can’t hurry it and you can’t wait forever,” said Anne Hollis, whose family has farmed here for four generations. A dry spell is forecast from Thursday.
