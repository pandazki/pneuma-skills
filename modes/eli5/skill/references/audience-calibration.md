# Audience Calibration

> The full taxonomy behind ELI5 Mode's ladder. Adapted from the upstream ELI5
> skill (DreambigOu/ELI5, MIT — see `../../NOTICE.md`), extended with the page
> visual register each audience's HTML page should carry.

This is the taxonomy; the *method* lives in SKILL.md under **Designing the page
for its reader**, and it is the load-bearing half. Derive your reader's page
there — the printed world it inherits from, its type pairing, measure, palette,
one gesture and decoration vocabulary, and on the other axis what convinces this
person, the shape their reasoning takes, how concrete the page has to be, what
its figures are for, and where the "so what" lands. Then open this file for the
nearest worked row and for the parts a method cannot give you: how much
vocabulary this reader carries, what ground is already familiar to them, what
they are accountable for.

Every row here hands you five things — **vocabulary ceiling**, **familiar
ground**, **framing**, **page visual register**, **what lands as
understanding** — and a page designed from those is a page for a person, rather
than for a general sense that somebody is "less technical".

Three rules cut across every row:

- **The reader is not the topic's junior.** They are an expert in something
  else who needs one thing from your field. A page that treats their unfamiliarity
  as a deficiency fails no matter how accurate it is.
- **An audience descriptor tells you what the reader already knows and how they
  read — not what the subject must be disguised as.** "A music major" says:
  university-educated, fluent with abstraction, no finance or engineering
  vocabulary, and an ear for rhythm and taste. It does not say "explain this in
  orchestras". Read every descriptor for its *prior knowledge, vocabulary
  ceiling, pace, and aesthetics* first; that reading is always the load-bearing
  one.
- **A comparison is chosen by the idea, not by the reader's résumé.** It does
  real work when the relationships match on both sides, and it strains when
  only the surface does. How much of it you need also moves with the reader:
  for a five-year-old the analogy *is* the translation, because the concepts
  themselves are missing; for an educated adult in another field usually only
  vocabulary and context are missing, and plain precise prose lands harder than
  a mapping table. The reader's own world is often the best place to look for a
  fitting structure — just not automatically the right one.

## Ages

| Audience | Vocabulary & sentences | Familiar ground | Framing | Page visual register | What lands as understanding |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Age 5** | Everyday words only. One idea per sentence. No term survives unless it is immediately re-said in kid words. | Toys, animals, snacks, the playground, hide-and-seek, tidying up a room. | "Here is a fun thing, and here is how it works." Curiosity, never obligation. | Rounded sans (Baloo 2, Nunito), body 22–28px, headings 48–72px, measure 24–34 characters, bright crayon palette on warm off-white, big SVG shapes, well under one screen of real text. | Seeing it happen to something they can name, in a story where somebody wants something. Narrative only — first, then, so. The picture carries the explanation and the words label it; they end on recognising a thing they had already noticed. |
| **Age 10** | Simple but not baby words. Cause and effect is fine — "because", "so that", "if … then". | School, sports, video games, collecting things, group projects, pocket money. | "How it works, and why it's clever." They will accept a mechanism if it has a payoff. | Rounded-to-geometric sans, body 20–22px at 1.65, saturated but slightly cooler palette, numbered step strip, a simple labelled diagram. | A mechanism they can follow step by step, with a payoff at the end. Cause → effect → why that is clever. One worked case they can re-tell, and a diagram that is a mnemonic rather than a decoration. |
| **Age 15** | Abstraction is fine. Real terms are fine when you define them once. Casual register — never *performed* casual. | Phones, social platforms, games, music, group chats, part-time work. | "This is actually how the thing you already use works." Respect, plus a hook. | Confident geometric sans (Space Grotesk, Archivo), body 18–20px, one loud accent used as a highlighter, pull quotes, chunky badges, skimmable sections. | The thing they already use turning out to work in a way they can now see, plus a number that surprises. Cause → consequence → the twist that explains the annoying part. Ends on what they can now notice or argue. |
| **Age 20–30** | Direct and unpadded. Common technical terms are safe; specialist jargon is not. | Rent, jobs, money, apps, commuting, planning a trip, splitting a bill. | "Here's what it is, here's what it means for you." Practical over theoretical. | Clean modern sans, body 17–19px at 1.65, measure 62–70, restrained contemporary palette, one clear figure that earns its space, medium density. | A concrete case with real numbers from a life like theirs. Claim → what it costs → what to do. One figure, load-bearing; the "so what" is a decision they might make this month. |
| **Age 40+** | Clear and complete. Respectful of long experience in other domains. | Home ownership, careers, managing a household or a team, insurance, long-term planning. | "This is the same problem you already solve elsewhere, in a different medium." | Humanist sans or warm serif, body 18–20px at 1.75, measure 58–64, calm neutral palette with one accent for the thing to remember; whitespace does the decorating and buys unhurried section breaks. | A precedent from a domain they have run themselves, and the trade-off stated honestly rather than smoothed. Narrative with one complication. Concrete cases beat abstractions; the "so what" is a judgement they are being equipped to make. |

**On age labels.** They are stand-ins for reading level and life experience, not
literal birthdays. A user asking for "an age 5 explanation" usually wants the
*delight and clarity* of that register — write the page a curious child could
follow and an adult would enjoy reading aloud.

## Grade / education levels

| Audience | Vocabulary ceiling | Depth | Framing | Page visual register | What lands as understanding |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5th grade** | Concrete words. Zero jargon — not "jargon with a definition", none. | The single core idea plus one consequence. | "Think of it like …" all the way through. | As Age 10: rounded sans, big type, bright, a step strip, one screen. | One story they could re-tell at dinner. Narrative, one chain. Every noun visible; the figures do half the explaining. |
| **Middle school** | Basic terminology allowed, each defined at first use. | Step-by-step logic; two or three linked ideas. | "First this happens, then this, and that's why …" | Geometric sans, body 19–20px at 1.65, colour-coded steps, a simple numbered flow diagram. | Following a chain themselves and predicting the next link. Step-by-step cause and effect; the flow diagram is load-bearing and the prose walks it. |
| **Senior high** | Proper terms, briefly explained. Moderate complexity is fine. | Mechanism plus one interesting complication. | "The real version has a wrinkle, and here it is." | Clean sans, body 17–18px at 1.6, measure 65–72, a modest accent, definition sidebars in the margin, one real diagram. | The wrinkle. Mechanism → complication → why the simple version is still useful. One worked example carried through, plus the case where it breaks. |
| **College student** | Technical terms with brief context. Theory *and* application. | Full mechanism, named concepts, one worked example. | "Here's the model, here's how it shows up in practice." | Academic-clean sans, body 16–17px, measure 70–76, restrained palette, a captioned figure, a small code or formula block where it earns its place. | The model and one instance of it, connected. Claim → evidence → implication, with the named concept given its real name. Figures are exact and captioned; the "so what" is being able to recognise the pattern elsewhere. |
| **Graduate school** | Full domain vocabulary. Defining basics wastes their time and reads as condescension. | Nuance, trade-offs, edge cases, failure modes, what the standard account gets wrong. | "You know the textbook version; here is where it is interesting." | Dense technical layout, body 15–16px, mono for anything symbolic, an explicit trade-offs table, references or footnotes; the tight setting buys full width for the table and the derivation. | Rigour. Assumption → derivation → limit, and the argument against the obvious alternative. One precise instance beats three loose ones; a diagram is a true model or it is omitted. The "so what" is intellectual — where the standard account fails. |

## Job roles

| Audience | They care about | Frame around | Page visual register | What lands as understanding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Manager** | Impact, timeline, risk, cost, who is affected. | Business outcomes and the decision in front of them. Lead with the answer; put the mechanism below the fold, or leave it out. | Memo-shaped: answer in the first screen, neutral professional sans (Inter, Source Sans 3) at 16–17px, measure 70–75, tabular figures, one accent and one warning tint, number tiles with real air around them, a ruled cost/benefit table, an ink-bordered decision box at the end. The restraint buys silence around the numbers. | Quantified impact with provenance, plus a precedent — what happened when someone else did this. Claim → evidence → implication → options with costs. Mechanism only where it changes the decision. Ends on an ask. |
| **Director** | Strategy, ROI, competitive position, resource allocation. | Big picture and second-order effects. One level higher than the manager page — less "what we should do this sprint", more "what this changes about our position". | Executive-brief: strong opening statement set two sizes up, 3–4 stat tiles, one strategic diagram, wide margins and short measure (60–66) so the page reads standing up. No implementation detail at all. | Second-order consequence argued from a small number of facts. Position → force acting on it → what changes → what it costs to be wrong. One figure, load-bearing. The "so what" is where the organisation ends up. |
| **Product manager** | User value, priorities, scope, what to build vs skip. | Feature impact and the boundary of the work. Say plainly what is cheap, what is expensive, and what is a trap. | Structured product-brief: crisp sans at 16–17px, a scope table (in / out / later), user-facing framing, one flow diagram, an explicit "what this costs us" section. | The boundary. Scenario → what the user gets → what it costs → what it forecloses. Concrete user stories over abstractions; the flow diagram is the argument about scope. Ends on a priority call. |
| **Engineer** | How it works, architecture, trade-offs, failure modes, maintenance cost. | Technical mechanics. Use real terminology — hedging it reads as being talked down to. Compare to structures they already know. | Precise: prose sans (Inter, IBM Plex Sans) at 15–16px beside a real mono at 13–14px, cool muted ground with a dark code surface, annotated code block, complexity notes, an explicit trade-offs table, inline SVG drawn to scale. The tight setting buys full width for code and diagram — they are the loudest things on the page. | Exactness. Assumption → derivation → limit, plus the trade-off matrix and why the obvious design was rejected. One real trace or query plan beats three sketches; a diagram must be a true model. The "so what" is craft — what to reach for, what breaks under load. |
| **Designer** | User experience, visual impact, flow, accessibility. | How it changes what the user perceives and does — latency, state, error surfaces, what becomes possible. | Visual-first: a defensible editorial pairing (display 40–64px against text 17–18px), a stated three-value palette on a visible grid, before/after and flow figures given real space, generous asymmetric margins. The page demonstrates the taste it discusses. | Seeing it. Perception → what produces it → what it opens or forecloses. Figures are the argument and the prose annotates them; states drawn to scale. The "so what" is what they can design now that they could not before. |
| **Colleague / teammate** | Practical context and what they need to collaborate. | How it affects their work specifically. Assume shared context; skip the preamble. | Working-doc: readable sans at 16–17px, measure 70–78, a "what changes for you" section, a short checklist, one diagram where there is a handoff to draw. Decoration is structure — headings and rules that let them find their own section in five seconds. | The diff. What was true before → what is true now → what they have to do differently. One concrete example touching their surface. The "so what" is their next commit. |
| **Executive / CEO** | The decision, the number, the risk. | One page, one thesis, one recommendation. | The most compressed rung there is: a headline claim at display size, three supporting numbers, one line of risk, one line of ask — set on a short measure with air around every figure. Anything longer is not this audience. | The number and its consequence. Thesis → three facts → risk → ask. No mechanism, no diagram unless it replaces a paragraph. The "so what" is a yes or a no. |

## Relationships

| Audience | Tone | Familiar ground | Page visual register | What lands as understanding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Partner (wife / husband / spouse)** | Warm, conversational, patient. The register of talking over dinner, not presenting. | Household tasks, shared routines, things you both did last week, money you manage together. | Warm text serif (Source Serif 4, Charter) at 19–21px / 1.8, measure 58–64, soft low-saturation palette on warm paper, roomy margins; the space buys a page that can be read across a table. Gesture: the opening line set large, in your own voice. | A story about the two of you and something you both watched happen. Narrative with one honest complication. Two everyday cases, unhurried. Concrete objects over abstractions; the "so what" is what to do and what to stop worrying about. |
| **Kids / children** | Playful and encouraging. Short. Let them feel clever at the end. | Games, cartoons, school, animals, building blocks. | As Age 5 / Age 10 depending on stated age: rounded type, bright palette, huge headings, a story strip, one meaningful emoji if it carries real meaning. | Seeing it happen. | **Friend** | Casual, occasionally funny, unpolished on purpose. | Pop culture, shared interests, "you know how …". | Relaxed modern sans at 17–18px / 1.65, measure 62–70, one personality accent colour, marginal asides, low formality — but still a designed page, not a text message. | The good bit first, then how it actually works, then the part that is funny or annoying. One shared example. The "so what" is something to say to somebody else. |
| **Client / customer** | Professional, reassuring, non-defensive. Especially when explaining a failure. | Their business, their workflow, the outcome they bought. | Polished neutral: restrained palette, clear headings at 16–17px body, a plain-language summary box up top, an explicit "what happens next" section. The restraint buys credibility — the page must look like it is not hiding anything. | A timeline they can verify, and a cause stated without hedging. What happened → why → what we changed → what you should expect. Numbers where a reassurance would otherwise float. The "so what" is what happens next, with dates. |

## Formations

A reader's training shapes a page more than their age or their job title does,
and it cuts across both — the music graduate might be twenty-two or sixty, a
manager or a freelancer. Two formations come up constantly and are badly served
by every row above, because both are educated readers who are nonetheless
strangers to your field.

### Arts / humanities-educated (music, literature, art history, philosophy, design history)

**What they bring.** Years of practice reading dense argument closely, comfort
with abstraction and ambiguity, a trained eye, and a low tolerance for prose
that has not been composed. **What they lack** is your field's vocabulary and
its sense of what is unremarkable — not the ability to follow it.

**Framing.** They are accountable for judgement and interpretation, so a page
that hands them a conclusion has taken away the part they value. Show the
reasoning being made: put the cases first and let the claim arrive as something
they have half-formed themselves. Terms get defined in the rhythm of the
sentence they appear in — a glossary box reads as a form to fill in.

**Page visual register.** The exhibition catalogue and the well-set poetry
collection are the ancestors. An editorial serif with real personality (Spectral,
EB Garamond, Crimson Pro, Source Serif 4) at 19–21px on 1.75 leading, measure
held at 62–66 characters, against a quiet sans (Inter, Work Sans) at 12–13px and
0.08em letterspacing for captions and folios; the contrast between those two
faces is the page's voice. Warm paper (`#faf7f2`), true ink, and one saturated
hue used *like* ink — one colour, roughly three appearances. Decoration is
typographic: a drop cap or small-caps opening clause, hairline rules, numbered
figure captions, real hanging quotation marks, and an asymmetric measure that
leaves a wide margin for sidenotes. No cards, no icon set, no rounded boxes —
those belong to a different printed world, and this reader can tell.

**What lands as understanding.** Worked cases that accumulate until the general
claim arrives already believed — three fully walked examples where a technical
rung would take one, each close-read for exactly what it shows. Case → case →
case → claim → its limit: exposition, complication, resolution. Images are
load-bearing here rather than ornamental; set them as plates with numbered
captions and discuss them in the text, so a reader who looks only at the plates
still follows the argument. They are paid in a lens they can carry elsewhere,
not in a task list, so the last beat is what this lets them see in their own
material.

### Practitioner (nurse, technician, electrician, chef, pilot, field engineer)

**What they bring.** Deep procedural expertise in a domain where mistakes are
expensive, a habit of reading under time pressure, and a fast, accurate sense of
when a document is wasting their time. **What they lack** is patience for
mechanism that does not change what they do.

**Framing.** They are accountable for an outcome in the next hour. Order the
page by when they will need each part, not by how the thing works, and let
theory in only where it changes an action. Authority with this reader is earned
by naming their real constraints — hands full, one pass, liability.

**Page visual register.** The discharge sheet, the checklist card, the field
manual. A high-legibility sans with unmistakable figures (Inter, IBM Plex Sans,
Atkinson Hyperlegible) at 17–18px on 1.55, step numbers large in a bold cut,
measure short at 50–60 characters so one step is one glance. White ground, black
text at maximum contrast, one alert hue reserved strictly for where it goes
wrong — spending that colour anywhere else is spending it. Decoration is rules
and blocks that fence one step from the next, a boxed stop-and-check panel, and
a labelled right-versus-wrong figure pair. Nothing here needs to be pretty; it
needs to survive a photocopy and a bad light.

**What lands as understanding.** What happens if I do X, and a case that went
wrong. Situation → action → check, with the signs that tell them it worked and
the ones that mean stop. Exact steps and exact numbers; figures are load-bearing
and show right beside wrong. The last beat is the next hour: do this, watch for
that, escalate here.

## Language calibration

Three broad modes cut across the tables. Once you know which one a page is in,
a lot of small decisions answer themselves.

### Simple audiences — young ages, family, non-technical roles

- **No jargon. Zero.** If a term is genuinely unavoidable, define it in the
  same breath and then keep using the plain phrase, not the term.
- **One idea per sentence.** Two clauses joined by "and" is usually two
  sentences pretending to be one.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** "The server is like a waiter who takes your
  order to the kitchen" beats "the server handles client-server communication",
  and it is not less true — it is the same truth at a resolution they can hold.
- **Use "you" and "your".** Second person turns a description into something
  happening to the reader.
- **Length is short.** A kid's page that runs three screens has already lost.

### Technical audiences — engineers, grad students

- **Use proper terminology.** Explaining what a hash map is to someone who
  writes them daily is the fastest way to lose their attention.
- **Spend the page on the interesting part** — trade-offs, edge cases, why the
  obvious design was rejected, what breaks under load.
- **Anchor to what they know**: "it's a B-tree, but the leaves are chained so
  range scans don't have to walk back up".
- **Be concise.** Respecting their existing knowledge is itself a form of
  clarity.
- **Be exact.** This is the one rung where an approximation reads as an error.
  Give real complexities, real names, real caveats.

### Business audiences — managers, directors, PMs

- **Lead with impact,** not mechanism. The first screen must be able to stand
  alone as the whole answer.
- **Quantify.** "Roughly 40% of peak-hour queries" is a fact they can act on;
  "a lot of queries" is not.
- **Skip implementation detail** unless it changes a decision. If it does,
  compress it to the sentence that changes it.
- **Frame as decisions.** End on "this means we should …", with the options and
  what each costs. A business page that ends on a description has not finished.

## Tone matching

| Audience band | Voice |
|---|---|
| **Ages 5–10** | Enthusiastic, like a favourite teacher who is genuinely delighted by the thing. "Oh, this one's fun." |
| **Teenagers** | Slightly casual, never performed. No adult impression of teen slang; the fastest way to be dismissed. |
| **Professionals** | Confident and clear. Bridge the knowledge gap without narrating the gap. |
| **Family** | Patient, warm, conversational — explaining over dinner, not from a lectern. |
| **Executives** | Decisive. State the conclusion, support it, stop. |

## Choosing the ladder

- **The user named one audience** → build a one-rung ladder. A single tailored
  page is a complete explainer; offer to add rungs afterwards rather than
  guessing at extras they did not ask for.
- **The user named several** → use their order if it is already simplest-first,
  otherwise reorder to climb and say that you did.
- **The user named none** → default to **age 5 → manager → engineer**. Those
  three sit far enough apart that the register shift is obvious, and they cover
  the three questions people actually have: *what even is this*, *what does it
  cost me*, *how does it really work*.
- **An audience with no row here** (a lawyer, a midwife, a war historian, a
  specific named person) → derive the row rather than borrowing the nearest one.
  Run both halves of *Designing the page for its reader* in SKILL.md: name the
  printed matter this person already trusts and inherit its type pairing,
  measure, palette and ground, one gesture and decoration vocabulary; then name
  what would convince *them*, the shape their reasoning wants, how concrete the
  page has to be and how often, what its figures are for, and where the "so
  what" lands. Read the descriptor for *reading*, never for costume — how much
  schooling and abstraction they bring, what they are accountable for (that is
  the framing), their vocabulary ceiling in *your* field. Their profession is a
  place you may borrow a comparison from when one is needed; it is not the
  language the page is written in. Write the derivation into the manifest
  entry's `tone` field so the next session inherits it.

## Quality check before you call a page done

- Does the first sentence say what the thing *is*, without machinery?
- Could this reader state the actual subject after reading — in its own terms,
  not only through whatever it was compared to?
- Is every comparison earning its place at the point it appears, and would this
  reader rather just be told the thing plainly?
- Is there a "so what" that names why *they* should care, in the currency this
  reader is actually paid in?
- Does the page carry the kind of evidence that convinces *this* reader — the
  worked cases, the exact mechanism, the number with a provenance, the story —
  rather than the kind you find easiest to write?
- Is everything on the page true — simplified, but not invented?
- Does the page *look* like it was designed for this person, not restyled from
  the rung next to it? Can you name its type pairing, its measure, its ground,
  and where its one gesture lives — and if it is a quiet page, what the quiet
  bought?
- Read side by side with its neighbour rung: is the difference obvious in the
  first two seconds? If not, one of the two is not committed to its register.
