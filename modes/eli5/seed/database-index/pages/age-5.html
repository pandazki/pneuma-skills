A big computer secret

How does a computer find things so fast?

It keeps a little list of where everything is.

Imagine your toy box

Every single toy you own lives in one giant box. Cars, blocks, dinosaurs, the little green frog — all of it, mixed up together.

Now someone says: find the red dinosaur.

1

You tip the whole box onto the floor. Every toy comes out. Every single one.

2

You start looking. Not it. Not it. Not it. Not it. Your knees hurt.

3

Somewhere near the bottom — there it is! Now you have to put everything back.

It worked. It just took forever.

Now imagine drawers

Same toys. But this time there are little drawers, and each one has a picture on the front. A car on this one. A dinosaur on that one. A block on the last one.

Find the red dinosaur? You look at the pictures, open one drawer, and there it is.

You didn't touch the cars. You didn't touch the blocks. You looked in one place, because the pictures on the front told you where to go.

So here's the secret

A database index is the little picture on the front of the drawer.

Computers have a REALLY big toy box

A computer keeps millions of things — every message, every order, every photo. When you tap a button, it has to go find one of them.

Without an index, it does the messy thing: it looks at every single one until it finds yours. That's a lot of looking!

With an index, it peeks at the little list first. The list says "that one is over there." So the computer goes straight there and gets it.

But there's a catch

Somebody has to make those little pictures. And every time you get a new toy, you have to add it to the right drawer — otherwise the list is telling fibs.

So the drawers are amazing for finding things, and a tiny bit of extra work for putting things away. Grown-ups who build computers think about that a lot.

Why you should care

When you open a game and your player is right there — no waiting, no spinny circle — that's because somebody made good drawers.

Tidy drawers.
That's the whole trick.
